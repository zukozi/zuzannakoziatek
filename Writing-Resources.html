Reading as a Lens Mini Lesson

What Is a Lens? 

A lens is a text that helps us see, interpret, question, or complicate something else. 

A lens helps us notice something new, challenge an assumption, deepen an interpretation, and ask better questions. 

Today's Goal: Use The Yellow Wallpaper as a lens. 

Our First Interpretation 

Virginia Slims Advertisement 

Question: What might someone conclude after looking at this advertisement? 

Women are becoming more independent; the ad celebrates women's progress; smoking is presented as a symbol of freedom; women have come a long way. 

Evidence from The Yellow Wallpaper 

"What is it, little girl?" 

"Bless her little heart." 

Question: What assumptions about women are revealed through John's language? 

Applying the Lens 

Revisit the slogan: "You have Come a Long Way, Baby." 

Before the lens: friendly, playful, encouraging. 

After the lens: potentially infantilizing; suggests women are still being talked down to; raises questions about equality. 

The Lens Move 

Original Interpretation (X): The Virginia Slims ad celebrates women's liberation. 

Revised Interpretation (Y): The ad celebrates liberation, but The Yellow Wallpaper suggests it may also reinforce assumptions that women are not fully equal to men. 

Your Task 

How does The Yellow Wallpaper deepen, challenge, or complicate your understanding of your chosen phenomenon from the medical advertisements collection? 

Ultimately, your group will have to Complete the following XY statement: The phenomenon of ____ is often understood as ____, but The Yellow Wallpaper suggests ____.

Course Summary: Medical Ads Patterns to Phenomena

This recap will help you understand what we discussed in class and, more importantly, the thinking process we practiced together. The goal was not simply to analyze individual advertisements but to learn how we move from observations to larger patterns and, eventually, to phenomena worth investigating.

Part 1: Revisiting Rhetorical Analysis

We began by revisiting several medical and pharmaceutical advertisements from the 1950s through the early 2000s.

As we looked at each advertisement, we continued practicing rhetorical analysis by asking:

  • Who is the intended audience?
  • What is the purpose of the advertisement?
  • What rhetorical choices stand out?
  • How do the visual and textual elements work together?
  • What values or assumptions does the advertisement promote?

Example: The “A New Experience” Halabar Advertisement (1955)

You quickly recognized that the intended audience was not the woman pictured in the advertisement but physicians who might prescribe the medication due to the ad being a direct mail sent to a doctor.

We also noticed several rhetorical choices:

  • A before-and-after visual of the same woman.
  • A darker image representing distress or nervousness.
  • A brighter image representing relief or happiness.
  • Pills placed between the two images, symbolizing transformation.

One student suggested that the advertisement portrays medication as a pathway to "a brighter life."

Together, the image and text create the message that medication can transform a woman's emotional state and improve her quality of life.

As a class, we concluded that the visual does not simply illustrate the text. Instead, it amplifies the text's claim about the effectiveness of the medication. That brought us back to multimodal composition and analysis.

Example: The “Now She Can Cope” Butisol Advertisement (1964)

We discussed that this advertisement has the same audience, though the reason is because it was published in the Medical Journal. The first thing we noticed was that a child appears to be tying up her mother with rope.

Students identified several interesting choices:

  • The child is dressed in dark colors.
  • The mother is portrayed more positively.
  • The image suggests that motherhood can be stressful or restrictive.
  • The medication is presented as a solution to everyday “situational” stress, which according to this ad, is motherhood.

One student observed that the child appears to be "tying her down," while another suggested the advertisement implies that mothers need medication to cope with daily responsibilities. Someone else also observed that the visual, what we are first drawn towards, is communicating ideas about motherhood before the audience even reads the advertisement's text.

This led us to think about what assumptions the advertisement makes about women and what assumptions the creators of the ad wanted physicians to make about women as well.

Example: The "Is She Just Shy?" Zoloft Advertisement (2003)

Students noted several rhetorical choices:

  • The woman is looking downward and appears isolated.
  • The image creates uncertainty.
  • The text asks, "Is she just shy, or is it social anxiety disorder?"

We discussed how the advertisement encourages physicians to interpret certain behaviors through a medical lens.

One student pointed out: "It's making her normal shyness more serious than it is."

Another student added: "It's like you need medication for something that isn't really a problem."

These observations became especially important later in our discussion.

Part 2: Identifying Changes in the Collection Through Time and Context

After analyzing individual advertisements, we began comparing advertisements across different decades, keeping in mind the different or same intended audiences.

Students noticed an important shift.

Earlier Advertisements (1950s-1960s)

Advertisements frequently used terms such as:

  • nervousness
  • tension
  • stress

Later Advertisements (1990s-2000s)

Advertisements increasingly used diagnostic language such as:

  • depression
  • social anxiety disorder

One student observed: "The later ads actually give a name to the mental illnesses, whereas the earlier ads just describe the experiences."

This observation helped us think about how definitions of illness and diagnosis change over time.

Part 3: Moving from Observations to Patterns

After discussing individual advertisements and noticing changes through the decades, we asked a new question:

What similarities do these advertisements share?

Students identified several recurring observations:

  • Many advertisements target women.
  • Many advertisements were written for physicians rather than patients.
  • Medication is repeatedly presented as a solution.
  • Happiness often appears as the desired outcome.
  • Certain emotions and experiences are portrayed as problems that need fixing.

Then we asked:

What larger pattern emerges from these observations?

One student responded:

"It's not okay to be shy."

"It's not okay to be nervous."

"It's not okay to have stress if you're a mom or just a woman in general."

This was an important moment because this student was no longer discussing a single advertisement. Instead, they were identifying a larger message that appeared across multiple advertisements.

Another student summarized this idea as:

"It's not okay to not be okay."

Together, we discussed how scholars might describe this larger pattern as:

The medicalization of women's experiences.

In other words, many ordinary emotions or experiences are portrayed as medical problems requiring treatment.

Additional Patterns Identified During Class

Pattern: Selling More Than Medication

One student noted: "They're selling something that isn't really necessary for the consumer."

This led us to discuss how the advertisements often promise much more than symptom relief.

Many ads appear to be selling:

  • happiness
  • confidence
  • fulfillment
  • a better life
  • normalcy

rather than simply selling medicine.

Pattern: Questioning Normality

Another student suggested that many of the experiences represented in the advertisements seem normal, but are presented as if they require treatment.

This led us to discuss a pattern involving:

The questioning of what counts as "normal."

Many of the advertisements encourage audiences to view ordinary emotions or behaviors as symptoms.

Pattern: Medical Authority

Students also noted that physicians are repeatedly positioned as experts who determine what is normal, healthy, or worthy of treatment.

This raised questions about:

  • medical authority
  • patient agency
  • pharmaceutical influence
  • trust in expertise

Part 4: Moving From Patterns Toward Phenomena

Once we identified patterns, we asked:

Why do these patterns keep appearing?

This question helps us move toward a phenomenon.

A phenomenon is a larger social, cultural, or historical process that helps explain why certain patterns continue to emerge.

Some examples we discussed included:

The Medicalization of Women's Experiences

The tendency to frame ordinary emotions and experiences as medical problems requiring intervention.

The Promise of Happiness Through Medication

The idea that medicine can provide not only health, but also happiness and fulfillment.

Medical Authority Over Our Bodily Autonomy

The influence physicians and medical institutions have in defining what counts as normal or abnormal.

Pharmaceutical Influence Over Medical Diagnoses

The growing role pharmaceutical companies play in shaping how people understand themselves and their experiences.

We also discussed how some patterns can function as phenomena themselves. For example:

The medicalization of women's experiences can be understood both as a recurring pattern across the advertisements and as a larger social phenomenon.

Part 5: Connection to Your Inquiry Notebook

The work we did in class is very similar to the work you will do in your inquiry notebook.

You begin by collecting observations.

For example:

I've noticed students apologize before asking questions.

Or:

I've noticed people constantly talk about being stressed while continuing to add more responsibilities.

Then you begin asking:

  • Students talk about being overwhelmed.
  • Students brag about being busy.
  • Students continue adding commitments.

↓

Phenomenon

  • Why is being busy valued?
  • Do students see busyness as a sign of success?
  • Where do these expectations come from?
  • How does social media influence this?
  • Who experiences this pressure most intensely?

Key Takeaway

The most important thing we practiced was moving beyond individual texts.

We began with rhetorical analysis of individual advertisements, but then we zoomed out to compare multiple advertisements and ask larger questions.

A helpful way to visualize the process is:

Observations --> Patterns --> Questions (Why, So What?) --> Phenomena

OR sometimes if the observations and pattern are rich enough,

Observations --> Patterns = Phenomena

The goal of inquiry is not simply to notice things. The goal is to begin identifying the larger ideas, processes, tensions, and phenomena that help explain why those observations keep appearing. That is the same process you will use as you continue developing your inquiry notebooks and working toward your lens-based essay.

A Century of Medical Advertisement Regulations

Before 1906 

Many medicines were marketed directly to consumers. Companies often made dramatic claims about what their products could cure, and there was little government regulation of advertising or drug ingredients.  

Early 1900s to 1970s 

As medicine became more professionalized, doctors and pharmacists became recognized as the primary medical experts. Pharmaceutical companies increasingly directed prescription drug advertisements toward physicians rather than patients. These advertisements often appeared in medical journals and professional publications.  

1950s-1970s 

Many advertisements for tranquilizers, antidepressants, and other medications were designed to persuade doctors about how patients should be understood and treated. Rather than speaking directly to consumers, these ads often framed medical professionals as the decision-makers.  

1980s-1990s 

Pharmaceutical companies began experimenting with direct-to-consumer advertising. In 1997, FDA policy changes made television advertising for prescription drugs much easier, leading to the widespread drug commercials familiar today.  

Today 

The United States is one of the few countries that permits widespread direct-to-consumer advertising of prescription medications. Drug companies spend billions of dollars annually advertising directly to consumers.  

Connection to Our Unit 

Question: If many of the advertisements we have been studying were created for doctors rather than patients, what assumptions do they make about who has authority to interpret and treat people's experiences?  

In the 1950s and 1970s, pharmaceutical advertisements often tried to persuade doctors how to understand and treat patients. Today, many prescription drug advertisements are aimed directly at consumers. How much has actually changed? 

Have consumers gained more control over decisions about their health, or are pharmaceutical companies still shaping how people understand their emotions, behaviors, and everyday experiences using similar tactics seen in the earlier ads? 

When we compare physician-directed advertisements to today's direct-to-consumer advertisements, do we see a shift in power, or simply a new way of influencing how people think about health, illness, and treatment? 

This question becomes especially interesting when read alongside The Yellow Wallpaper, a story in which a physician's authority shapes the narrator's life and treatment.
