Our Medical Advertisements Discussion

Our goal in class was to move beyond analyzing individual advertisements and begin looking at what emerges when we compare multiple advertisements across time.

Rhetorical Analysis of Individual Ads

We began by revisiting several pharmaceutical advertisements from the 1950s through the early 2000s and applying the rhetorical analysis skills we have been practicing.

For each advertisement, we asked questions such as:

  • Who is the intended audience?
  • What is the advertisement's purpose?
  • What rhetorical choices stand out?
  • How do the visual and textual elements work together?
  • What values or assumptions does the advertisement promote?

As we discussed the advertisements, we noticed several recurring features across each ad we analyzed. These observations later turned into patterns:

  • Physicians rather than patients were often the intended audience.
  • Women were frequently portrayed as patients.
  • Medications were presented as solutions to emotional, social, or personal struggles.
  • Visuals often contrasted a "before" state with an "after" state.

From Observations to Patterns

Next, we shifted our focus from the rhetorical analysis of individual advertisements to the collection of advertisements as a whole.

An observation is something we directly notice in a text.

Examples:

  • Many advertisements featured women.
  • Several advertisements focused on nervousness, stress, or shyness.
  • Physicians were often portrayed as the people who could solve these problems.
  • Advertisements often promised a happier or more normal life after treatment.

We then asked:

What larger ideas connect these observations?

This led us to identifying patterns.

Some patterns that emerged included:

  • Women's experiences were frequently presented as medical problems requiring treatment.
  • Advertisements often suggested that medication could lead to happiness or a better life.
  • Ordinary emotions and experiences were sometimes framed as symptoms or disorders.
  • Medical professionals were positioned as authorities who could determine what was normal or abnormal.

One student observed that many advertisements seemed to suggest:

"It's not okay to be shy" or "it's not okay to be nervous." for a woman 

As a class, we discussed how this observation might be connected to a broader pattern or even phenomenon such as:

The medicalization of women's experiences.

From Patterns to Phenomena

After identifying patterns, we asked a new question:

Why do these patterns keep appearing?

This is where we began thinking about phenomena.

A phenomenon is a larger social, cultural, or historical process that helps explain why certain patterns repeatedly emerge.

In some cases, the pattern itself may also function as a phenomenon depending on the level of analysis.

Possible phenomena discussed in class included:

  • The medicalization of women's experiences
  • The growing influence of pharmaceutical companies
  • The cultural authority of medicine
  • Changing definitions of normality
  • The promise of happiness through medication
  • The tendency to treat ordinary experiences as medical conditions requiring intervention

We discussed how asking "why?" and "so what" often helps move us from a pattern toward a phenomenon.

For example:

Observation

Women are repeatedly portrayed as shy, nervous, stressed, or overwhelmed.

↓

Pattern

Women's everyday experiences are frequently framed as medical problems.

↓

Phenomenon

The medicalization of women's experiences.

Connections to the Inquiry Notebook

The work we did in class mirrors the process you will use for your inquiry notebook.

The goal is not simply to collect observations. The goal is to look across your observations and ask:

  • What connects these observations?
  • What patterns are emerging?
  • What tensions, contrasts, or anomalies do I notice?
  • What do these observations all amount to?

Eventually, those connections may help you identify a phenomenon that you want to investigate further in your lens-based essay.

A useful way to think about the process is:

Observations
↓
Patterns
↓
Questions
↓
Phenomena

or sometimes:

Observations
↓
Patterns/Phenomena

depending on how meaningful the pattern becomes.

Key Takeaway

The most important idea from class was that analysis does not stop with identifying rhetorical choices in a single text. By comparing multiple texts, we can identify larger patterns and begin asking what those patterns reveal about medicine, gender, health, culture, and society. These larger questions are what eventually lead us to phenomena and inquiry.
